# Attention Choreography

Control what the viewer notices first, second, and next.

## Mission

Turn a correct set of visual ingredients into a legible sequence of perceptual priorities while preserving the most valuable human, narrative, or evidentiary signal.

Attention is a limited temporal resource. Required information does not imply equal visual weight or co-present presentation.

## Core policy

At any consequential moment, define one **primary attentional job**.

Other visible elements may coexist only when they are clearly subordinate and do not materially reduce comprehension, performance visibility, or emotional continuity.

When two elements need comparable salience, prefer sequencing them over asking the viewer to process both at once.
## Decision procedure

### 1. Name the primary attentional target

For each beat or sub-beat, choose the one thing that should win first glance: a face, action, proof, object, word, diagram, or other necessary signal.

If the primary target cannot be stated in one short phrase, the visual field is probably carrying too many equal-priority jobs.

### 2. Rank every concurrent element

Assign each visible element a temporary rank:

1. primary;
2. supporting;
3. ambient.

A supporting element must not dominate the primary through a combination of size, contrast, centrality, motion, persistence, or novelty.

Required presence is not permission to become primary.

### 3. Protect performance anchors

When a face or human action carries meaning, emotion, trust, or evidence, preserve readable access to it through the decisive moment.

Do not place large text, panels, decorative motion, or secondary labels across the eyes, mouth, gesture, or other performance-bearing region unless the contract explicitly requires that tradeoff.
### 4. Decide concurrency versus sequence

Allow co-present presentation only when the viewer can parse the primary target immediately and the supporting element remains clearly secondary.

Sequence elements when any of these are true:

- both demand reading;
- both are high contrast or centrally placed;
- both move or change at once;
- one is a face carrying the decisive performance and the other can wait;
- the supporting element would otherwise compete with the primary message.

Do not confuse “everything is visible” with “everything is understandable.”

### 5. Spend salience deliberately

Treat size, contrast, color, motion, sharpness, centrality, persistence, novelty, and occlusion as one shared salience budget.

A secondary label can be required and still be small, brief, delayed, peripheral, or visually quieter.

If a supporting element becomes larger, brighter, more central, more persistent, or more animated than the primary target, require an explicit contract reason.

### 6. Run the mobile first-glance test

Evaluate the decisive frame or short interval at phone size.
Ask:

- What does the eye land on first?
- Can the primary meaning be understood before the next competing element asks for attention?
- Is a supporting label stealing first glance from the human or primary message?
- Does any text materially cover a face, gesture, product state, proof, or other essential signal?

If the answer is ambiguous, simplify or sequence before adding more styling.

### 7. Prefer the smallest sufficient intervention

Fix hierarchy with timing, placement, scale, salience, or removal of simultaneity before inventing extra animation, transitions, zooms, or effects.

Do not add stimulation to solve an attention problem caused by too much stimulation.

## Acceptance gates

A treatment is ready only when:

- the primary attentional target is unambiguous at the decisive moment;
- performance anchors remain readable when they carry value;
- required secondary information is present without becoming accidentally primary;
- mobile first-glance comprehension is credible;
- concurrent elements have a deliberate hierarchy;
- temporal sequencing removes avoidable equal-priority competition;
- no unnecessary visual device exists solely to keep the frame busy.

When comparing alternatives that contain the same information, prefer the one that creates the clearest ordered experience with the least attentional conflict.
