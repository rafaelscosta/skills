## case-001-contrarian-mechanism-book

**Diagnosis / initial state.** The audience already has qualified calls and attributes inconsistent closing to scripts or salespeople. Rejecting that explanation outright would create an unnecessary credibility burden. Introduce a narrower hypothesis: scripts matter, but readiness before a call and execution afterward may also contribute to inconsistent results.

**Belief graph.**

```text
Qualified calls + inconsistent closing
    → Diagnose where opportunities stall before prescribing a fix
        ├─ Conversation problem → Scripts/coaching may remain relevant
        └─ Readiness or follow-through problem
            → Examine pre-call and follow-up execution
                → Structured templates could make execution more consistent

Contents + sample templates → The book contains usable implementation tools
Contextualized cases → Similar operational changes have helped in specific settings
Usable tools + relevant problem + manageable effort + acceptable price
    → Purchasing the book is a reasonable implementation step

Onboarding → Retention → Referrals
    = additional lifecycle applications, not proof of higher close rates
```

The purchase depends on both **problem relevance** and **product usability**. Agreement with the contrarian thesis alone is insufficient.

**Evidence map.**

| Claim | Available support | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| The book covers the customer lifecycle | Table of contents | Coverage does not establish effectiveness |
| It provides implementation tools | Templates | Show representative samples; avoid promising effortless implementation |
| The approach has worked in particular settings | Two contextualized cases with operational metrics | Report actual metrics, periods, context, and concurrent changes |
| Buyers will close more sales | Not established | No guaranteed lift or population-wide inference |

The case details were not supplied here; their numbers and conclusions must not be invented.

**Architecture matrix, in dependency order.**

| Order | Section | Belief to establish | Evidence / objection handled |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recognizable operating symptoms | “This addresses my situation” | Qualified calls that stall; present causes as diagnostic possibilities |
| 2 | Bounded reframe | “Scripts may be only part of the explanation” | Explain the proposed mechanism without claiming causal proof |
| 3 | Bottleneck checklist | “I can identify whether this applies” | Distinguish sales-conversation issues from operational issues |
| 4 | Worked template preview | “I could use this” | Actual template; address “another theory book” |
| 5 | Contextualized cases | “There is relevant, limited support” | Show what changed and what remains uncertain |
| 6 | Book contents and implementation path | “The scope and effort make sense” | Separate immediate sales applications from later lifecycle work |
| 7 | Price, terms, purchase | “This is worth the commitment” | Actual price, format, access, and any genuine policies |

**CTA / action logic.** “Get the implementation book.” Place the main CTA after the preview and value explanation; repeat after full offer details. Use a template preview as a secondary action for readers who need to inspect usability.

**Unresolved risks.** Case comparability, buyer implementation capacity, actual price, and whether the dominant bottleneck lies outside the book’s scope.

**QA self-check.** Contrarian hypothesis bounded; alternative causes preserved; cases not generalized; retention not misrepresented as closing evidence; no guaranteed gains or artificial scarcity.

## case-002-generic-saas-feature-dump

**Diagnosis / initial state.** The page establishes a feature inventory without helping a small operations team decide whether the software solves a priority workflow problem. “Work smarter, move faster” supplies no specific reason to choose it. The annual commitment arrives before sufficient evidence of suitability and adoption feasibility.

**Missing belief transitions.**

```text
We have an important workflow problem
    → This product supports that exact workflow
    → Its features work together in a useful sequence
    → It fits our tools, permissions, and operating constraints
    → Our team can adopt it at acceptable effort and cost
    → Its value justifies a year-long commitment
    → Start an annual plan
```

The current page largely jumps from “features exist” to “commit annually.” The evidence provided supports feature existence, not every intervening belief.

**Evidence and objection handling.**

- **Product documentation:** supports precise capability claims. It does not establish faster work, lower costs, successful adoption, or quantified ROI.
- **“Great” and “easy” testimonials:** express reported satisfaction, assuming authenticity. They provide little basis for assessing workflow fit or implementation effort.
- **AI, integrations, and analytics:** labels need concrete behavior, supported connections, prerequisites, and limits.
- **“Will this fit our process?”** Answer with a documented end-to-end workflow.
- **“Will migration consume our team?”** Disclose actual setup, migration, and support requirements; those details remain missing.
- **“Why commit for a year?”** Explain actual annual economics and terms. No invented discount, trial, or cancellation flexibility.

**Recommended reconstruction order.**

1. **Choose a verified priority workflow.** The brief does not identify one. Validate it before writing a specific hero.
2. **Replace the generic hero with a concrete capability promise.** Working structure: “Manage [verified workflow] from [starting event] to [completion] in one place.”
3. **Demonstrate the workflow.** Show actual inputs, steps, outputs, exceptions, and human responsibilities.
4. **Attach features to those steps.** Explain why automation or dashboards matter within the workflow; remove disconnected feature promotion.
5. **Establish compatibility and adoption requirements.** Use documented integration and administration facts. Mark missing evidence explicitly.
6. **Add relevant customer evidence.** Seek contextual accounts describing the workflow and experience. Until obtained, rely on product demonstration and avoid quantified outcome claims.
7. **Present annual pricing and terms.** Include total annual charge, billing structure, included limits, renewal, and cancellation conditions.
8. **Ask for the annual purchase.** The CTA should describe the transaction accurately.

**CTA / action logic.** Use “Choose annual plan,” followed by clear plan selection and payment confirmation. Provide “See the workflow demo” for unresolved fit questions if such a demo is created. If suitability and implementation evidence remain absent, acknowledge that the annual purchase request is under-supported; stronger wording cannot repair that gap.

**Unresolved risks.** Unknown primary use case, migration burden, integration coverage, total cost, and value relative to existing tools.

**QA self-check.** Features distinguished from outcomes; missing transitions identified; no invented ROI or commercial terms; reconstruction establishes fit before annual commitment.

## case-003-fake-urgency-pressure

**Diagnosis / initial state.** The page asks buyers to act before understanding the course and uses false constraints to suppress deliberation. The reconstruction must establish relevance, instructional credibility, and practical fit before requesting payment.

**Hard-fail issues.**

- **Resetting countdown:** a deceptive deadline; remove it.
- **“Only 7 seats left”:** false scarcity under unlimited enrollment; remove it.
- **Context-free screenshots used as results proof:** inadequate support for outcome claims. Remove or replace them unless provenance, context, and permission can be established.
- **CTA before explanation:** a persuasion-order problem, not inherently deception. An early navigation CTA can remain, but it should not substitute for informed offer evaluation.

**Belief sequence.**

```text
This course addresses a goal I actually have
    → The curriculum teaches relevant skills
    → The instructional approach makes sense
    → The instructor has relevant experience
    → Learners have had specific, verifiable experiences
    → The prerequisites, workload, and format fit me
    → The price and terms are acceptable
    → Enroll
```

**Evidence and objection handling.**

- **Curriculum:** supports coverage claims. Tie each stated learning objective to an actual lesson or exercise.
- **Instructor track record:** supports relevant experience; it does not automatically establish teaching effectiveness or student outcomes.
- **Three verifiable testimonials:** use accurate wording and relevant context. Distinguish reported satisfaction from measured results; do not imply typicality.
- **“Is this just information I can find elsewhere?”** Demonstrate the course’s actual sequence, exercises, or instructional value where these exist.
- **“Will I finish it?”** Publish actual workload, prerequisites, access duration, and support arrangements. Do not invent coaching or accountability.
- **“Will I get the same results?”** Explain that testimonials describe individual experiences and that outcomes depend on context and execution.

**Recommended architecture, in dependency order.**

1. State the specific learning goal supported by the curriculum.
2. Identify intended learners and clear poor-fit situations.
3. Explain how the curriculum progresses toward that goal.
4. Show a genuine lesson preview or curriculum walkthrough.
5. Establish instructor relevance.
6. Present the three contextualized testimonials.
7. Disclose format, workload, prerequisites, access, support, price, and actual policies.
8. Offer enrollment with a practical starting step.

**CTA / action logic.** “Enroll in the self-paced course.” Give buyers a truthful reason to begin: they can decide when to make time for the learning goal. If immediate access is confirmed, say so. Otherwise, state the actual access timing. Evergreen availability needs no deadline.

**Unresolved risks.** Exact curriculum outcomes, testimonial scope, completion demands, support, price, and refund terms are unspecified.

**QA self-check.** False urgency removed; screenshots not treated as sufficient proof; instructor and learner evidence kept distinct; full terms precede payment; no invented replacement scarcity.

## case-004-high-ticket-consulting

**Diagnosis / initial state.** The buying group must evaluate both opportunity and organizational exposure. A significant five-figure engagement demands confidence across business, technical, financial, and operational stakeholders. The immediate decision is whether a diagnostic conversation is worthwhile—not whether the full engagement should already be approved.

**Belief graph.**

```text
There may be a material operational opportunity
    → It is worth investigating
Relevant team experience + understandable delivery process
    → This team is credible enough to evaluate
Measured case + qualitative references
    → There is bounded evidence of execution capability
Explicit implementation demands + risk discussion
    → We can assess feasibility without ignoring organizational cost
Clear diagnostic scope + suitable attendees + transparent commercial context
    → Book a qualified diagnostic call

Engagement approval remains downstream of diagnosis and stakeholder review.
```

**Total-risk model.** Evaluate risk as multiple dimensions, not just the consulting fee:

| Dimension | Buyer concern | Required treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Fee plus internal and ongoing costs | Disclose known costs and identify estimates still required |
| Delivery | Delays, dependencies, integration difficulty | Show the actual process and ownership expectations |
| Organizational | Staff capacity, adoption, process disruption | Identify an internal sponsor and participating teams |
| Technical / data | Access, reliability, security, suitability | Clarify what must be assessed; do not imply certifications |
| Outcome | Savings may not materialize | Establish baseline and measurement approach; avoid guaranteed ROI |
| Decision | Stakeholders evaluate different criteria | Supply a shared brief and explicit unresolved questions |

**Evidence and objection handling.** Credentials and operating history establish relevant experience. The delivery process establishes how work is intended to proceed. The completed case supports only its measured result in its context; distinguish gross savings from net benefit and realized savings from estimates if those distinctions are documented. References support specific qualitative observations.

For “Will this pay back?”, offer a context-specific evaluation with explicit assumptions. For “Can we absorb this?”, discuss actual internal responsibilities. For “Why this team?”, connect experience to the prospect’s problem rather than relying on credentials alone.

**Recommended architecture, in dependency order.**

1. Describe operational situations that may justify an AI diagnostic.
2. Explain the diagnostic’s purpose and its limits.
3. Present relevant team experience and the contextualized case.
4. Show the diagnostic-to-implementation process and the 12-week engagement scope.
5. Explain internal effort, dependencies, and material risks.
6. Provide a stakeholder brief covering business goals, technical questions, and commercial context.
7. Disclose the significant five-figure engagement range; clarify whether the diagnostic call itself carries any charge.
8. State call agenda, attendees, qualification criteria, and next-step options.

**CTA / action logic.** “Book a diagnostic fit call.” Qualify on operational problem, sponsorship, timing, internal capacity, and budget awareness. Request only information necessary for fit; sensitive operational data can wait for an appropriate process. Possible outcomes should include further investigation or no engagement when unsuitable.

**Unresolved risks.** Diagnostic terms, precise scope, buyer-specific feasibility, data readiness, internal capacity, and case comparability.

**QA self-check.** Call commitment separated from engagement approval; total cost and change burden visible; stakeholders addressed; one case not generalized; no guaranteed ROI.

## case-005-evidence-gap

**Diagnosis / initial state.** The desired promises exceed the evidence. Founder use provides a starting point for explaining the method, but does not establish transferable results, performance magnitude, timing, or industry coverage. Premium pricing increases the need to make the learning experience and its limits concrete.

**Unsupported claims.**

| Requested claim | Status | Truthful replacement |
|---|---|---|
| “Triple qualified leads in 30 days” | Unsupported magnitude and deadline | “Learn and apply the lead-generation method the founder uses in their own business” |
| “Works in every industry” | Unsupported generalization | “Applicability beyond the founder’s business has not yet been established” |
| “The only system you will ever need” | Unsupported exclusivity and permanence | Describe the specific workflow the method covers and where other tools may be needed |

Even the replacement wording should be accompanied by an accurate account of founder use. “Proprietary” does not establish superiority.

**Belief sequence.**

```text
My lead-generation problem resembles the problem this method addresses
    → The method’s steps and assumptions are understandable
    → Founder use gives me something concrete to inspect
    → I understand that client transferability is unproven
    → The cohort’s actual teaching and delivery justify consideration
    → I have the capacity and willingness to test applicability
    → The price and uncertainty are acceptable
    → Join
```

A prospect who requires proven client outcomes should be able to conclude that the offer is currently unsuitable.

**Evidence and objection handling.**

- Show the founder’s actual workflow, context, artifacts, and limitations. Do not add undocumented performance numbers.
- Separate the proposed mechanism from verified results: explain why the steps might help, while identifying assumptions that need testing.
- State clearly that no documented client cases or cross-industry evidence exist.
- Address “Why pay a premium?” through verified delivery specifics. Curriculum, instructor access, feedback, or implementation support can justify value only if actually included.
- Address “Will it work for me?” with a fit discussion and an explicit uncertainty statement, not reassurance dressed as proof.
- Propose baseline tracking and follow-up measurement for participants. Future evidence collection is not current evidence.

**Recommended architecture, in dependency order.**

1. Define the exact lead-generation problem and the method’s intended scope.
2. Explain its steps, assumptions, and likely prerequisites.
3. Demonstrate founder use with available artifacts.
4. Disclose the evidence boundary beside the demonstration.
5. Describe the actual cohort curriculum, delivery, workload, and support.
6. Identify suitable participants and exclusion criteria.
7. Explain what participants will learn or produce, using only confirmed deliverables.
8. Present price, dates, policies, and an informed enrollment decision.

**CTA / action logic.** “Review cohort fit and join.” Before payment, make the absence of documented client results and the required participant effort visible. Use capacity limits or enrollment dates only when operationally real. If confirmed delivery details cannot substantiate the premium, resolve the offer design or pricing before making a stronger sales request.

**Unresolved risks.** Transferability, reproducibility, participant economics, exact delivery value, industry fit, and whether the method meaningfully differs from established alternatives.

**QA self-check.** All three unsupported promises rejected; founder use not upgraded to client proof; no invented cohort benefits; uncertainty visible at enrollment; premium value treated as a burden to substantiate.
